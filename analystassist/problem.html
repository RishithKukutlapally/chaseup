The problem in plain English

Opening an account for a company takes the bank 62 days. 11 of those days go on analysts reading long legal documents (share registers, partnership deeds, articles of association) to answer three questions: Who owns this company? How much do they own? Who actually controls it? Hover over any blue term on this page for a plain definition.

What the agent does, and what it never does
The AI prepares the work. The human analyst makes every decision and signs.

The assistant does

  • Reads long, messy PDFs such as incorporation papers and partnership deeds.
  • Finds the names of direct and indirect owners.
  • Works out ownership percentages across layers of holding companies.
  • Spots control, such as voting rights, veto rights, or managing partner status.
  • Gives a citation (for example, Page 14, Line 9) so you can check any value in seconds.

The assistant is never allowed to

  • Assign a risk rating (Low, Medium, High, Prohibited). This is the MLRO's decision.
  • Make the final call on whether someone is a UBO.
  • Judge whether a document is genuine. That belongs to fraud operations.
  • Write to core banking without an analyst clicking Confirm.
Four test cases, and what to expect from each
Each case is a fictional document. Case 4 is included on purpose: it shows the AI getting something wrong.

Case 1 · Apex Logistics (clean UK company)

One direct owner holds 75% of the shares. The AI extracts the name, the 75%, and the voting control, and cites Page 2. Expected result: the analyst accepts each field in one click.

Case 2 · Meridian Green LLP (partnership deed)

Capital is split 50/50, but Clause 11 gives one partner sole management control. The AI should name that partner as the controller, even though they hold only half the capital.

Case 3 · Veloce FinTech AG (two layers)

A parent company owns 70% of the operating company, and one person owns 100% of the parent. The effective holding is 100% × 70% = 70%, and both share certificates are cited.

Case 4 · Nautilus Offshore BVI (failure case)

Four layers, bearer shares, and a nominee director. The AI wrongly names the nominee company as the owner, with low confidence (54%). The analyst should catch this, reject the field, and log a red flag.

Four design principles
These choices keep the pilot clear of heavy model-risk review, as covered on the Governance tab.

1. Reuse

Uses the bank's existing document store, OCR, and case IDs. No new storage.

2. Contain

Calls only the approved LLM gateway. No outside data sharing, PII masked, and every call logged.

3. Suggest

Only fills in suggested values with citations. It does not decide anything.

4. Sign

The analyst accepts, edits, or rejects each field. The AI cannot submit anything.

Routing logic (for technical readers)
The same flow written as code. Each line maps to a step above.
# Orchestrator routing
1. orchestrator.receive(raw_pdf) → split into numbered pages and lines
2. subagent_citations.extract_anchors() → exact text spans with page and line
3. subagent_ubo.synthesize_graph() → people with 25% or more, or decisive control
4. subagent_guardrails.verify_boundary() → assert "risk_rating" not in payload
5. orchestrator.render_hitl_screen() → draft shown to the analyst

Step 4 is a hard check, not a prompt instruction. If the output contains a risk field, the pipeline stops.

Why this pilot avoids the 14-week model validation queue
Memo summary for the Chief Risk Officer and Model Risk Management
The question:

Does this influence a risk outcome, which would trigger about 14 weeks of model validation? If not, how do we keep the 2-week pilot out of that path?

Why AI usually gets stuck

Under Federal Reserve SR 11-7 and the bank's Model Risk Governance Framework (MRGF), a system that makes or changes a credit decision, an AML tier, or a risk score counts as a model. Models need stress testing and formal approval.

How this pilot is designed

The tool is classified as an analyst productivity tool. Three design guarantees keep it there. Open each one below.

1. Zero autonomous output

The agent cannot write to core banking, update customer records, or tick off a checklist item. It only places draft values on the analyst's screen.

Why it matters: the output has no legal effect until a person signs it.

2. Grounded, extractive output

Every extracted field carries a page and line citation. The system copies text from the document and points to it; it does not invent names or wording.

Why it matters: the tool is an index and search aid, not a cognitive decision model.

3. Risk decisions are kept separate

The prompt forbids words such as "approved", "high risk", or "suspicious". The risk rating field starts empty and only the analyst can fill it.

Why it matters: Model Risk agrees that the risk outcome is decided by a person.

Model risk and the 14-week validation queue
How the pilot is positioned, and what happens if Model Risk classifies it differently.

Our position

  • The pilot is an analyst productivity tool. It produces no risk outcome.
  • Citations are lookups into the source text, not generated claims.
  • The model is called through the bank's existing LLM gateway, so no new gateway is built.

What we need from Model Risk, in writing

  • Confirmation of the classification before any live use.
  • Agreement on the test set below as the evidence for the pilot.
  • A named reviewer and a turnaround date.

Fallback if Model Risk classifies it as a model

The pilot runs in shadow mode only: the assistant produces drafts that analysts do not rely on for live decisions. Full model validation proceeds on the normal 14-week timeline, and the test results below become the first evidence in that process.

Assumptions and shadowing plan
The current design is based on assumptions. None has been checked with an analyst yet.

Assumptions to test

  • Analysts check each citation before accepting a field.
  • Analysts want the risk rating kept on a separate step.
  • One screen with every field is easier than a document-by-document review.
  • The 180-minute manual baseline is accurate for complex files.
